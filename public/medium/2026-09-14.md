Ashok Khade was born around 1955 in Ped, a village in the Tasgaon area of Sangli district, Maharashtra, into a Chamar family — a Dalit community that sat at the bottom of the caste hierarchy his village still enforced. His father mended shoes for a living. His mother worked as a daily-wage agricultural laborer for upper-caste landowners. There were six children in the family, and by every account, there wasn't always enough food to go around.

At some point in his childhood, his father moved the family to Mumbai, still working as a cobbler — reportedly plying his trade under a tree near Chitra Talkies, a well-known old cinema hall. Ashok attended a charity-run school. None of this reads like the beginning of a story about a man who would eventually run a company with thousands of employees. That's the point of telling it.

### Fifteen years at Mazagon Dock

In 1975, Khade joined Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders — one of India's most significant state-owned shipyards — as an apprentice draftsman. He stayed for roughly fifteen years, earning a diploma in mechanical engineering along the way and working his way up to an executive role in quality control.

Sometime in the 1980s, Mazagon Dock sent him to West Germany as part of a team working on a submarine construction project — Mazagon Dock's real submarine collaborations in that period involved West German shipbuilding technology transfer, part of India's broader effort to build indigenous submarine capability. What exactly Khade did on that assignment isn't detailed in the record available today, but the effect it had on him is consistent across every account: it was his first real exposure to how a different industrial system worked, at a different scale, with different expectations of what an engineer from India could build. People who tell this part of the story treat it as the moment the idea of starting his own company stopped being unthinkable.

### 1991 opened a door, and he and his brothers walked through it

For most of Khade's career at Mazagon Dock, India's oil and gas services sector was effectively closed to private companies — a state-dominated industry with little room for outsiders. That changed with the economic liberalization of 1991, which opened offshore oil services to private players for the first time.

Khade left Mazagon Dock and, together with his brothers Datta and Suresh, founded a company they named DAS Offshore Engineering — the initials, put together, spelling out the three brothers' first names. It's a small detail, but it says something: this wasn't a company built to be a personal vehicle. It was built as a shared bet among three brothers from the same background, taking the same risk together.

Early contracts reportedly ran on credit and on the goodwill Khade had built over fifteen years at Mazagon Dock — a network of relationships that doesn't show up on a balance sheet but clearly mattered enormously in the company's first years. DAS Offshore grew into fabrication and refurbishment work for offshore oil and gas platforms, jackets, and subsea pipelines, eventually working with clients that included ONGC, L&T, Essar, and Hyundai.

### From three brothers to thousands of employees

By the early 2010s, DAS Offshore Engineering had grown to more than 4,500 employees, with annual turnover reported at the time above $32 million. More recent profiles, written around Khade's 2026 Padma Shri, describe the business in the range of ₹350–500 crore — using different metrics from different years, which makes an exact current figure hard to pin down precisely, but the scale of the trajectory is not in dispute: a company built by three brothers from a family with no business background, no capital base, and no institutional backing, grew into an employer of thousands.

In 2026, the Government of India awarded Ashok Khade the Padma Shri — one of the country's highest civilian honors — in the Trade and Industry category. He has also been profiled in Milind Khandekar's book *Dalit Millionaires*, alongside a small number of other entrepreneurs from Dalit backgrounds who built substantial businesses in a country where caste has historically shaped who gets access to capital, contracts, and credibility in the first place.

### Why the ordinary parts of this story matter

It would be easy to tell this story as a single leap — poor cobbler's son becomes industrialist — and skip past everything in between. But the actual shape of Khade's career is the opposite of a leap. Fifteen years at one shipyard, rising slowly through technical and then managerial roles. A multi-year posting overseas that changed his sense of what was possible, but didn't hand him a company. A regulatory door that happened to open in 1991, which he and his brothers had to actually walk through, with their own credit and their own relationships, no guarantee of success attached.

None of this is a story about a single dramatic moment. It is a story about fifteen years of unglamorous shipyard work being the actual foundation for what came after — proof that the years that don't look like progress are often exactly where the real groundwork gets laid.
